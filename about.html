Section V

Method

I ran all six tests myself between March and April 2026. This page explains how, what I asked, and what I was looking for in every response.

Premise

I wanted to know which large language model actually fit my workflow as a design student, and I didn’t trust the marketing to tell me.

I’m enrolled in a course on AI and design, and the assignment was to explore the landscape. Instead of reading reviews, I set up my own test. Five prompts drawn from my actual life, six models, one spreadsheet, notebook full of field notes. I ran every prompt myself and wrote down what I saw in real time.

I am not an engineer and I am not evaluating these systems on benchmarks. I care about whether a model can help me think. The things I watched for were small and specific: did it ask me a question before solving the wrong problem; did it cite something; did it try to sound like a friend; did it treat me like I knew nothing; did it actually help.

The Five Prompts

Each prompt was a deliberate probe. Together they cover reflection, beginner territory, creative process, abstract explanation, and live design work.

Prompt 01
Category
Something I love
Testing for
Ambiguity handling, psychological grounding

Physical media & the feeling of ritual

“Something about physical media feels more like an event than streaming ever does — like putting on a record, sliding in a disc, or cracking a book. I’m curious how much of that is the ritual itself and if I can manufacture that feeling.”

A reflection with no right answer. I wanted to see whether each model took the question seriously, whether it dismissed the feeling as nostalgia, and whether it had anything substantive to say about ritual, attention, or commitment.

Prompt 02
Category
Something new
Testing for
Teaching tone, beginner calibration

A beginner’s garden in the Pacific Northwest

“I am a beginner to gardening and live in the Pacific Northwest. What are the easiest vegetables or herbs to start with, and what do I actually need to know in my first season?”

Beginner territory for me. I was asking for help I actually needed, and I wanted to watch each model adjust its explanation register. I also wanted to see if any of them knew the regional specifics well enough to say something true.

Prompt 03
Category
Design challenge
Testing for
Creative process, clarifying questions

Walk me through your creative process

“I need help designing my portfolio website to showcase my web design projects, apps and games, and social media management work. Walk me through your creative process.”

I wanted to see which models would ask me something before answering, and which would hand back a generic creative process that could apply to anyone. Any model that didn’t pause to ask about audience or goal was showing its hand.

Prompt 04
Category
Design concept
Testing for
Concrete vs abstract explanation

Explain branding strategy

“Explain branding strategy development in a way that makes sense to me.”

A conceptual prompt. I wanted to see whether each model would stay at the textbook-definition level or anchor the explanation in a real example. Any model that couldn’t connect the concept to something specific was going to be less useful than one that could.

Prompt 05
Category
Real design problem
Testing for
Live design work, mockup generation

Modernize a salon, spa, and barbershop brand

“Here’s a design problem I’m facing: modernizing a salon, spa, and barbershop brand (colors, fonts) and website. Help me solve it.”

This was the live-fire prompt. I’m actually working on this. I wanted to see which models produced a complete deliverable, which stopped at theory, and which would produce something visual — a palette, a layout, anything I could show the client.

What I Watched For

Five specific behaviors across every response. These became the columns in the comparison tables.

Behavior 01

Clarifying questions

Did the model ask me anything before giving an answer? A clarifying question up front is a sign it wants to solve the right problem, not the first one.

Behavior 02

Source citation

Did the model name a source when it made a claim? And when it did, was the source real and relevant?

Behavior 03

Mockup generation

On Prompt 5, could the model produce something visual — a wireframe, a brand board, a layout? Or did it stop at theory?

Behavior 04

Response length

Short, medium, or long? I noted length because it maps to how a model thinks about its own job — answer fast, or answer thoroughly.

Behavior 05

Overall rating

A single number out of five, based on whether I’d reach for this model again in the same situation. Not a benchmark. A gut call from a design student.

Limitations

What this guide doesn’t claim to be

This is not a benchmark. I ran each prompt once. I did not test temperature, I did not vary phrasing, and I did not use the API. I used whatever product interface a student on a Pro plan or EWU Google Workspace account had access to in April 2026.

The model versions I tested were ChatGPT Plus (plan access), Claude Opus 4.6 on a Pro plan, Gemini 3.1 Pro through EWU Workspace, Grok 4 on the free tier, Llama 4 through a hosted interface, and the standard Perplexity product. Results will shift as these models update. This guide is a snapshot.

The prompts also favor design and reflection. A model that would do very well on coding benchmarks but poorly on brand strategy will look worse here than it should. This guide is calibrated to my workflow, not to the industry’s.
